*This piece was also published on Law360: [State Bars Need To Get Specific About AI Confidentiality](https://www.law360.com/articles/2460723).*

Despite the avalanche of articles published about artificial intelligence use in the legal industry, a threshold question remains unresolved: Is it safe to put client information into AI tools?

But the answer won't come from a published article or a court decision. It is state bars that need to move beyond reasonableness standards to provide specific, actionable guidance — because to get the full value of AI tools, lawyers need to input actual client information.

Ultimately, state bars must evaluate the three frontier AI models — ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini — assess each plan tier, and tell lawyers which ones are safe for client information.

## Waiting to adopt technology has a cost.

Past technology transitions in law came with a forgiving timeline. Firms slow to adopt Westlaw spent more time in the library. Firms slow to move to the cloud were slightly less nimble. The laggards caught up without meaningful consequence.

Those transitions were about efficiency at the margins — doing the same work, slightly faster. AI changes what lawyers can deliver and how fast they can deliver it.

Proficiency with AI isn't a switch you flip. It's built through months of hands-on use, developing judgment about where the tools excel and where they fall short. Lawyers building fluency now will have an edge over those who wait.

Put simply: Clients will choose lawyers who use AI because those lawyers will do more work, faster, at higher quality.

But all this value requires putting actual client information into these tools. Generic prompts produce generic output. The real leverage comes when the actual contract, the facts and the actual dispute are in the conversation. And right now, many lawyers do not see those benefits because they're concerned it's unsafe.

## The profession is divided and uncertainty is holding one side back.

No software is completely safe. That's always been true. Deciding whether a tool is safe enough for client information has always been a risk-based judgment, not a binary one. The profession had the same debate about email in the 1990s and cloud storage in the 2010s. In both cases, it eventually converged on an understanding of what was acceptable.

For AI, that analysis has a natural starting point: Does the tool train on your data? Once training is off, the remaining evaluation is essentially the same as for any other software: contractual protections, security certifications and data-retention policies.

Many sophisticated organizations — including major law firms — have already concluded these tools are safe for sensitive information. Many are using standard commercial plans. Across the profession, lawyers are quietly doing the same. But many others are stuck.

Consider the solo attorney who wants to use AI. They read the articles. They understand the potential. They begin researching plan options for Claude or ChatGPT, see the varying security features, and freeze. If they want to put client information in, is Claude's Team plan good enough? Do they need the Enterprise plan? They're not sure, and they're not willing to risk an ethics complaint to find out.

## Current state bar guidance is well intentioned, but not sufficient.

To their credit, state bars have clearly signaled that lawyers can use AI.[^1]

The Florida State Bar's January 2024 ethics opinion opens with: "Lawyers may use generative artificial intelligence in the practice of law."[^2] The Illinois Supreme Court's AI policy says AI use "may be expected, should not be discouraged, and is authorized provided it complies with legal and ethical standards."[^3] The Texas Committee on Professional Ethics warned in a February 2025 opinion that lawyers should not "unnecessarily retreat[] from the use of new technology that may save significant time and money for clients."[^4]

But when it comes to using AI with confidential information, state bars get vague. They have largely applied the same approach they've used for every previous technology wave: They remind lawyers of their ethical obligations, instruct them to exercise reasonable judgment, offer a multifactor balancing test, and suggest due diligence on vendors.[^5]

The closest thing to a concrete rule that emerges from bar guidance is that you should not put client information into a tool that trains on your data.[^6] Beyond that, state bars give lawyers standards, but no practical sense of what meeting them looks like.

In guidance released in 2023, the California State Bar instructs that "a lawyer must not input any confidential information of the client into any generative AI solution that lacks adequate confidentiality and security protections," but never defines what "adequate" means.[^7]

In a November 2024 ethics opinion, the Mississippi State Bar acknowledged the tension directly, noting that lawyers should avoid putting identifiable information into AI tools, but conceded that, "depending on the tool, this might not be possible as the effectiveness of some [generative] AI tools depends on receiving client information."[^8]

The truth is, bar guidance regarding what is "safe enough" has never been particularly useful. This was OK when the profession had time to slowly converge, but that's no longer the case now that AI is involved.

## Some guidance goes further, but not far enough.

Some state bars have supplemented their official positions with more detailed, practical guidance. These efforts deserve real credit.

In October 2025, Illinois' Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission released a guide on implementing AI that classifies both data sensitivity and tool safeguards across four tiers.[^9] It maps them against each other. It names ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini specifically. And it states plainly what most guidance leaves implicit: Once you reach the business or enterprise tier, the AI-specific risk — training on your data — is contractually eliminated, and the remaining analysis is essentially the same as for any other cloud software.

Utah took a more direct approach: In January, the state bar approved LexisNexis as its official "trusted AI partner," and built a resource hub around Lexis+ AI.[^10] But it's a vendor partnership, not a formal ethics determination, and it doesn't directly address whether lawyers can use it with confidential information.

Through its LegalFuel Practice Resource Center, the Florida Bar's Special Committee on AI Tools and Resources published a guide in March that cuts through the ambiguity more directly than anything else available. It tells lawyers plainly: Free AI models may train on your data — don't use them for client work.[^11] Its comparison chart lists confidentiality as an explicit criterion, marks which tools meet it and links to each vendor's security documentation. A solo practitioner reading this guide has a good idea of what to do.

But none of these documents is official guidance. What's missing is bars willing to put their formal authority behind a clear answer.

## State bars must act now.

The goal isn't to resolve every AI confidentiality issue at once. It's to ensure that every lawyer has access to at least one AI tool they can confidently use with client information.

The best place to start is with the three frontier models that most lawyers are considering: ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. Each has a well-documented plan structure.

Take Claude as an example. There are three commercial plans to choose from: Team, self-serve Enterprise, and sales-assisted Enterprise. All three contractually prohibit training on client data.

A state bar could tell lawyers: The Team plan is sufficient for most solo practitioners and small firms; the self-serve Enterprise plan is the right choice if a client requires documentation of how their data was handled, or if you have custom retention requirements; and the sales-assisted Enterprise plan is required only if you need a business associate agreement for information covered by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, or custom contractual data protection terms.

State bars should do the same for ChatGPT and Gemini, put the guidance on a website, and commit to reviewing it quarterly.

These evaluations would also create a form of precedent. Practitioners could compare the protections in evaluated tools against others they're considering.

Some will object that state bars shouldn't pick winners. But evaluating the primary platforms lawyers are already using isn't picking winners, it's acknowledging reality. Others will say products change too quickly. But the solution is periodic reviews, not paralysis.

## Don't let fear hinder progress.

Confidentiality is at the heart of the legal profession. It's the foundation of the attorney-client relationship. State bars are right to protect it. But we cannot let fears about confidentiality prevent the profession from moving forward.

AI should be a democratizing force. A solo practitioner with the right tool can do in hours what once required a team. But that potential only materializes if lawyers can use tools with client information. Right now, the confidentiality question is one of the biggest barriers to unlocking this potential.

State bars are in a unique position to make that potential real. Translating complex, technical questions into clear professional obligations — so individual lawyers don't have to navigate them alone — is precisely what bar guidance exists to do. This is the moment to do it.
